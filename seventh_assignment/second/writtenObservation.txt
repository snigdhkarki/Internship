Key Findings & Observations
1) The "Combined" Trap: Putting all the cities into one histogram completely ruins any chance of a clean bell curve. You get multiple random peaks (a multimodal distribution) simply because you're forcing vastly different climates—like desert heat and chilly maritime weather—into the exact same bucket.
2) Steady vs. Wild Swings: The KDE curves reveal exactly how volatile each city is. Places with ocean-regulated climates (like London) have very tall, narrow curves, meaning their weather stays highly predictable. Shifting continental or desert climates have wide, flat curves, showing a much broader temperature swing.
3) No Real Outliers: Even using the strict $1.5 \times IQR$ rule, you won't find many true statistical outliers in a standard 7-day window. Weather moves in broader weekly systems, so a single day rarely behaves like a wild anomaly unless a massive storm front hits.
4) Clean, Symmetric Data: For most of these cities, the mean and median numbers are practically identical (well within 1°C). When those two metrics line up that perfectly, it tells you the weekly data is beautifully balanced with no weird, one-off spikes pulling the average away.
5) The Rain Effect (Bonus): If you layer the rainfall data over the temperature charts, a clear pattern jumps out: rainy days equal flatlined temperatures. The heavy cloud cover blocks the sun and the evaporation keeps things cool, which instantly shrinks the temperature variance for that city.
